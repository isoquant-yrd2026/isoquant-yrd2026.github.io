Session A · Lecture Hall · Ground floor

Quantum Gases & Correlated Matter

  1. 14:00
    Sarah Görlitz
    Three-particle bound states in core-level spectroscopy
    Institut für Theoretische Physik
    Abstract

    Few-body bound states are important throughout physics, from hadrons to semiconductors. Their formation is determined not only by the binding particles but predominantly by their surroundings. In doped semiconductors an exciton can capture a second carrier to form a trion, bound by tens of meV. Core-level excitations seem to pose a similar problem at a different scale: absorption creates a tightly localized hole and a bound electron, while the valence band can supply an additional carrier. We report the core-level analogue of a trion in bulk SrTiO₃ at the Ti L edge. The x-ray absorption spectrum shows seven sharp peaks, understood from atomic multiplet theory as a Ti 2p core hole interacting with a Ti 3d electron in a cubic crystal field. Fluorescence and Auger-Meitner decay calculations reproduce the linewidths of the first four peaks but predict far sharper features than observed for the highest two, which are degenerate with the electronic continuum. Ab initio GW+BSE reproduces the multiplet result with seven infinitely sharp excitons. In contrast, an embedded impurity model, which includes dynamical charge transfer from the O 2p band, yields a finite width for the two highest states. This disagreement indicates their trion nature: two Ti 3d electrons bound to a single Ti 2p hole, leaving a delocalized hole in the O 2p valence band. To test how generally this mechanism affects core-level spectra, we present preliminary toy-model calculations. With this we aim to better understand the interaction of core-excited states with their environment.

  2. 14:30
    Lukas Schulz
    Signatures of Goldstone modes in the Bogoliubov–de Gennes excitation spectrum of trapped supersolids
    Physikalisches Institut
    Abstract

    Supersolids combine the frictionless flow of superfluids with periodic density modulations and are predicted to exist in a wide variety of structurally distinct phases. Experimental realizations have achieved 1- and 2-dimensional droplet lattices and the two-dimensional stripe phase. By simultaneously breaking a global U(1) symmetry associated with superfluidity as well as translational invariance due density modulations, supersolids, unlike spatially homogeneous BECs, support excitations below the lowest trap frequency. Here we discuss tools for identifying low energy Goldstone modes in a harmonically confined stripe phase supersolid. In particular, we present first experimental results of a 164Dy supersolid excited through simultaneous ramps of the contact interaction strength and dipole orientation alongside numerical simulations of the same process. The numerical simulations are supplemented by solving the parity projected Bogoliubov-de Gennes equations on ground states obtained through imaginary-time evolution of the extended Gross–Pitaevskii equation. This provides a controlled framework, enabling the identification of three distinct low-energy modes associated with translational, rotational, and compressional excitations.

  3. 15:00
    Christian Gölzhäuser
    Competing structural orders in dipolar supersolids
    Physikalisches Institut
    Abstract

    Dipolar quantum gases have allowed the discovery of novel many-body states (e.g. supersolids), while at the same time providing an outstanding degree of control and tunability. In our experiment at the Physical institute we aim to study the behaviour of dipolar quantum gases of dysprosium in low dimensions and uniform tailored potentials. Here we report on our investigations on structural phase transitions both from a uniform to a density modulated state by tuning the s-wave scattering length and between a triangular crystal lattice modulation and a stripe-like modulation. We are able to observe the stripe modulation by tilting the orientation of our dipoles in plane through an external field. In both cases of the modulation we are able to observe phase coherence in a small range of scattering lengths below the superfluid to supersolid transition. We aim to study the nature of the different transitions to and within the density modulated regime as well as the excitation spectra of the different modulated phases.

  4. 15:30
    Brian Bostwick
    What can a mixture tell us about quantum fields?
    Kirchhoff-Institut für Physik
    Abstract

    Multi-component experiments allow inquisition into imbedded systems, broadening the scope from single-component measurements. This new facet of imbedding may help answer questions regarding how and in what ways can systems become entangled via a bath? We will have a guided discussion regarding what parameters one could hope to measure from a imersued motional two-level system.

Session B · Seminar Room 1 · Ground floor

Particles, Fields & Detectors

  1. 14:00
    Ekaterina Vlasiuk
    Resolving Higgs–Goldstone coupling with two-dimensional spectroscopy
    Institut für Theoretische Physik
    Abstract

    Effective field theories describe the low-energy dynamics of diverse quantum many-body systems in terms of collective excitations and their interactions. Experimentally identifying these interactions remains challenging: spectral broadening can arise from coupling to other modes, but also from disorder or spatial inhomogeneity. Can two-dimensional spectroscopy selectively reveal interactions between collective modes and uncover their forms? I will discuss the basics of nonlinear two-dimensional spectroscopy and present our work on interacting amplitude (Higgs) and phase (pseudo-Goldstone) modes. Using the Keldysh formalism, we study an effective O(N) field theory in which a Higgs excitation couples to pairs of pseudo-Goldstone modes, using a bilayer antiferromagnet as a concrete realization. The coupling generates a rephasing echo in the third-order response. Its asymmetry distinguishes Higgs decay into pseudo-Goldstone pairs from additional broadening mechanisms and provides access to the interaction strength. I will then discuss possible applications to Bose gases, Bose–Einstein condensates and impurity systems. I would welcome discussion of experimentally accessible protocols and extensions to other interacting field theories.

  2. 14:30
    Humaira Nazneen
    Investigating Infrared Scintillation in Xenon for Rare-Event Detector Research
    Max Planck Institute for Nuclear Physics/Heidelberg University
    Abstract

    Xenon scintillation is widely exploited in rare-event experiments, particularly in searches for dark matter and other weakly interacting phenomena. While ultraviolet emission is extensively studied and routinely used, the infrared emission channel remains underexplored. A better understanding of its formation mechanism, temporal behaviour, and sensitivity to pressure and purity conditions is essential to access its full potential. This work investigates IR scintillation in xenon using a dedicated optical setup equipped with UV- and IR-sensitive photomultiplier tubes. The temporal relationship between the two emission channels is studied through UV–IR time-difference distributions, while the characteristic timescale of the delayed IR signal is evaluated using multi-exponential fits. Controlled injections of molecular impurities, including O₂, N₂, CO, CO₂, CH₄ and SF₆, are used to investigate changes in the observed emission dynamics and thus constrain possible formation mechanisms. The measurements reveal differences in the effects of various molecular impurities on the IR emission dynamics, providing constraints on possible formation pathways. In light of recent theoretical work, a transition involving an excited ionic xenon dimer is considered as a candidate mechanism for the IR continuum (Hammann et al., 2026: link ). The observed impurity dependence is being investigated in this context, including the possible role of charge-exchange processes, given the latest observation of the effect of impurity species varying with their respective ionization potentials. These measurements provide an experimental characterisation of an underexplored xenon emission channel and offer new constraints on its possible microscopic origin, contributing to the understanding of scintillation processes relevant to future rare-event detectors

  3. 15:00
    Konrad Kockler
    High density QCD and the moaty endpoint
    Institut für Theoretische Physik
    Abstract

    Whether the QCD transition ends in a critical endpoint at high baryon density is a central question for heavy-ion collision experiments. Physics at high density is out of reach for lattice QCD because of the sign problem. This region also hosts a moat regime, where the pion energy is lowest at a finite momentum instead of at rest, which undermines the approximations of most other calculations. We compute the QCD phase diagram with functional QCD, in a framework that includes the moat self-consistently. This allows us, for the first time, to compute the critical endpoint directly within the moat regime, instead of inferring it by extrapolation from the crossover. Beyond the endpoint, we find signatures of inhomogeneous instabilities at higher densities.

  4. 15:30
    Thomas Hepworth
    The Coldest SUN in the Universe: The Highest Density Source of Ultracold Neutrons
    Physikalisches Institut Heidelberg University/Institut Laue-Langevin
    Abstract

    Ultracold neutrons (UCN) are a unique probe for fundamental physics via long-duration measurements. This allows high-precision neutron EDM (nEDM) experiments, among other science cases. The PanEDM collaboration aims to improve the limit on the nEDM by a factor of 10, by exploiting the new UCN source SuperSUN at the ILL. SuperSUN delivers high stored densities of very low-energy UCN, enabling longer storage times and higher-precision experiments. UCN data is often analyzed by empirical models and fit parameters. This limits interpretability of measurements, projecting away information of well understood particle level dynamics. We present a physical inference on UCN storage and accumulation data allowing the reconstruction of in-situ total energy spectrum and associated physics. The model will be presented alongside data, and possibilities to further the understanding of downstream experiments in the context of nEDM systematics.
